Wobble Finds Another Way

A story and a screen-free game about getting stuck, asking for help, and trying a different way.

A Lego tower falls. A drawing does not look the way a child imagined. A homework question refuses to make sense.

Sometimes the disappointment stays with the task. Sometimes it becomes something much bigger: "I am no good at this."

It is tempting to answer with encouragement: "Keep trying. You can do it." But what if the child has already tried the same thing several times? What if they need company, a break, or another approach before another attempt?

As a father and someone who works with systems, I like the idea of finding another route. Not as a way to turn children into machines, but as a simple picture we can share: a blocked path does not mean there is something wrong with the traveller.

That is the idea behind Wobble. Below is a story to read together, followed by a screen-free activity you can try. It is not a tested parenting method, and I am not presenting it as something that has already worked with my children. It is an invitation to play and talk.

The Story: Wobble Finds Another Way

Read together, and pause whenever your child has a question or an idea.

In the foothills of Gear Valley lived a little orange scout robot named Wobble. He had blue lights for eyes, rubber treads, and a basket that smelled of cinnamon.

Every morning, he carried buns from the bakery to the mountain research station. He knew the route: past the Whispering Pines, over the stone bridge, then up the sunny path.

For twenty-five mornings, it worked.

Then came Tuesday.

A storm had swept through the valley overnight. When Wobble reached the bend, he stopped behind the warning fence. The bridge was gone. Below it, brown water rushed past broken stones.

Wobble checked his map. Across the bridge. He checked again. Across the bridge.

"But there is no bridge," he said.

He stayed safely behind the fence, but his thoughts went round and round. The buns would be late. Everyone was waiting. He had one job, and now he could not do it.

"Perhaps I am not a very good scout."

A robin landed on a nearby signpost. "You sound upset."

"I know this route," Wobble said. "I should know what to do."

The robin stayed beside him. For a while, neither said anything.

Then she asked, "Does your map show anything else near the station?"

Wobble was not ready to look. "In a minute."

"All right," said the robin.

After a while, Wobble opened the wider map. Beyond the bakery was a long service road that curved around the hillside. He had hardly noticed it before because the bridge route was shorter.

"This road reaches the station too," he said. "But I do not know whether the storm damaged it."

He called the station. The caretaker had checked the service road that morning: it was open, with a firm surface and no river crossing.

"Take your time," she told him. "We can wait for breakfast."

Wobble marked the bridge as closed and traced the longer route with a green line. He turned towards the bakery, then rolled onto the service road.

At each bend, he slowed down and checked the ground ahead. He could stop and call for help if anything looked unsafe. He did not have to guess.

By the time he reached the station, the buns were no longer warm. Wobble was muddy and still a little disappointed about being late.

The caretaker opened the door. "There you are. Come inside."

While she warmed the buns, Wobble saved the new route on his map. Tomorrow, he would check whether the bridge was still closed before setting off.

He had not fixed the bridge. He had found a way to deliver breakfast, with a little help.

The Game: Find Another Route

If your child would enjoy being Wobble, allow about fifteen minutes for this activity. Try it during a relaxed moment, not as a surprise lesson in the middle of an upset.

Set Up Together

  • You need: Two or three lightweight boxes or cushions, paper, a marker, and a toy to deliver.
  • The route: Choose two floor-level spots in one clear room. One is the bakery; the other is the station. Keep the route away from stairs, sharp corners, kitchens, and anything slippery.
  • The roles: One person delivers the toy; the other helps plan. Swap roles if you like. Keep eyes open and move at an ordinary walking pace. You can also move a toy robot around a tabletop map instead.

Play in Four Steps

  1. Make a plan. Draw a simple route together and follow it once. No exact step counts or robot vocabulary needed.
  2. Change the route. While everyone is stopped, place a box across part of the path. Explain that the route has changed. Leave a clear, safe way around it.
  3. Notice the obstacle. Stop before the box. Try saying, "That way did not work. Can we try something else?" A disappointed sigh is allowed too.
  4. Choose what comes next. Let your child suggest a route, ask for help, or take a break. Draw the new path and try it together. Stay on the floor: no climbing over furniture or squeezing underneath it.

If frustration takes over, stop playing and attend to that first. There is no penalty or restart for having feelings, and no need to finish the delivery today.

When you swap roles, let your child place the obstacle while you are stopped. You can model not knowing immediately: "Hmm. My first plan will not work. I need a moment to look." Leave room for them to offer ideas rather than performing a perfect solution.

Afterwards, choose one question, not a quiz: "What helped when the route changed?" Or ask whether there was anything they would change about the game. Their answer might be about the boxes, not a life lesson. That is fine.

What I Hope Stays With Them

The useful part of the robot metaphor is small: a plan and the person using it are not the same thing. We can change one without judging the other.

Children are not systems to repair. A game cannot remove the hurt of getting something wrong, and finding a detour is not always possible straight away. Sometimes the next step is asking someone to explain a question differently. Sometimes it is resting and returning tomorrow.

I would like the story to make one ordinary sentence easier to say:

"This way is not working yet. Can you help me?"

There is a lesson for us as parents too. The robin does not rush Wobble, hand him the answer, or insist that he cheer up. She notices, stays nearby, and asks a question when there is room for one.

Next time a child pushes their work away, perhaps we can start with "That looks frustrating" instead of another instruction. Then listen. They may want help, company, or some space before they can think about another approach.

The point is not to get a smiling child back to work as quickly as possible. It is to let them know they do not have to figure everything out alone.

What helps your child when their first plan falls apart? And what helps you resist the urge to solve it for them?
