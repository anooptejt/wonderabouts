Can You Find
Another Route?

One toy delivery. A surprise obstacle. More than one way to get there.

In Wobble Finds Another Way, a missing bridge changes a breakfast delivery. This time, you get to draw the map. You can read the story first or simply start playing.

Before you start: Pick a relaxed moment. This is not a test, a race, or a way to stop someone feeling upset. There is no score and no penalty for needing a break.

Gather your supplies

  • Two or three lightweight boxes or cushions.
  • Paper and a marker.
  • A toy large enough to be safe for everyone taking part.
  • A clear, level area in one room.

Choose a bakery spot and a delivery spot on the floor. Stay away from stairs, kitchens, slippery surfaces and sharp corners. Keep eyes open, walk normally, and never climb over or crawl beneath furniture.

Tabletop option: Move a toy along a paper map instead of walking. A grown-up can draw and move the pieces with a younger child. Keep small loose parts away from young children.

Ready? Let’s try.

  1. Draw your first route.
    Mark the bakery and the station. Draw a path between them. Follow it once with your toy.
  2. Change something.
    With everyone stopped, place a box across part of the path. Explain what has changed. Leave a clear, safe route around it.
  3. Pause and notice.
    Stop before the obstacle. Try saying, “That way didn’t work. Can we try something else?” There is no countdown.
  4. Choose your next move.
    Draw another route, ask for help, or take a break. When you are ready, try your plan. Swap roles so everyone gets a turn to set the challenge.

Choose your kind of adventure

These age ranges are starting suggestions, not rules. Adapt to the child, their interests, and how much support they want.

Ages 3–5 · Move a toy together

Use a simple tabletop map with one large obstacle and two clear choices. Let a grown-up read, draw and guide. Point to the route together. There is no need to explain a lesson.

Ages 6–9 · Be the route finder

Draw your own map, make the delivery and swap roles. Can you find two safe ways around the same obstacle?

Ages 10–13 · Add a constraint

Make two delivery stops. Add a pretend muddy patch that must be avoided. Compare two routes: which is shorter, and which has more room? Explain your choice rather than looking for one “correct” answer.

Teens & grown-ups · Design the challenge

Invent a delivery mission with a limit: perhaps a robot has enough pretend battery for ten map squares. Test it with a partner. If it is impossible, change the rules or the route. Discuss what information you needed before committing to a plan.

One question is enough

“What helped when the route changed?”

Or ask, “What would you change about the game?” Listen to the answer, even if it is about a funny-looking box rather than problem-solving.

If frustration appears, attend to the child before the mission. Company, help and stopping for today are all valid options.

The engineering connection

A small trial, noticing what happened, and adjusting the next attempt are ideas Anoop borrows from engineering practice. Here they are a playful analogy, not a claim that children think like machines or that this activity has proven developmental benefits.
